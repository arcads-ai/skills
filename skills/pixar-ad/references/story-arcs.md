# Story Arcs

The 15-second skeleton in SKILL.md tells you the beat positions. This tells you
what to put in them.

Contents: the five arcs · choosing an arc · the two-character rule · worked
example · beat-writing craft · what fails

---

## The five arcs

Each is a shape, not a template. Pick the one the product's real benefit fits,
then cast it from the product read.

### 1. THE THING I CAN NO LONGER DO
The character has lost access to something they love. Not a task — something
tender. Reading to a child, threading a needle, seeing a handwritten letter.

- **Low point:** they make an excuse to cover it. This is the beat that works.
- **Best for:** age-related products, mobility, vision, hearing, dexterity.
- **Why it converts:** the buyer is often the adult child watching, not the user.
- **Trap:** any hint of a medical or disease claim. Keep it to ordinary ageing.

### 2. THE ONE I LEFT BEHIND
The character is absent and something they love is alone. The product is how they
stay present.

- **Low point:** the one left behind gives up waiting.
- **Best for:** cameras, monitors, pet tech, smart home, anything remote.
- **Why it converts:** guilt is the strongest purchase driver in this register.
- **Trap:** a cute device watching a person reads as surveillance. Point the
  product's attention at what the owner loves, never at a person, and keep the
  emotional point of view with the owner.

### 3. THE SAME EVENING, AGAIN
A small daily failure that repeats. The product breaks the loop.

- **Low point:** the moment of recognition that this is every night.
- **Best for:** routines, meals, chores, sleep, anything habitual.
- **Why it converts:** the viewer recognises their own week.
- **Trap:** at 15 seconds you cannot show repetition. Imply it in one line of
  dialogue ("Again?") rather than trying to cut a montage.

### 4. THE SECRET KEPT
The character is hiding a struggle from someone who would help. The product lets
them stop hiding, or lets the other person help.

- **Low point:** the lie, delivered kindly.
- **Best for:** anything with embarrassment attached.
- **Why it converts:** relief from shame is stronger than relief from effort.
- **Trap:** do not make the character pathetic. Dignity throughout.

### 5. THE HANDOVER
Someone gives the product to someone else. The gift IS the story.

- **Low point:** belongs to the recipient, before the gift.
- **Best for:** any product where reviews show the buyer is not the user.
- **Why it converts:** it shows the purchaser their own role in the story.
- **Trap:** the giver should stay small on screen. A hand and a voice is often
  enough, and it saves designing a third character.

## Choosing an arc

Work backwards from the product read.

| The read says | Arc |
|---|---|
| Reviews mention buying it for a parent or spouse | 5, or 1 with a handover turn |
| Benefit is about absence, distance, being away | 2 |
| Benefit is about a recurring daily friction | 3 |
| Benefit removes embarrassment | 4 |
| Benefit restores a lost capability | 1 |

Arcs 1 and 5 combine well: the loss is established, the handover is the turn.
That combination was the strongest structure this skill produced.

## The two-character rule

One character alone can only be sad. Two characters make a relationship, and a
relationship is what the viewer buys.

The second character does not need lines, a face, or screen time. A child
waiting. A dog by a door. A hand entering frame with a voice attached. What
matters is that the low point costs the lead something **in front of someone
else** — private failure witnessed is dramatically stronger than private failure
alone.

The second character also gives you the payoff for free: their reaction at 0:11
is the proof the product worked, and it needs no dialogue.

## Worked example — reading glasses, arc 1 + 5

Product: high-magnification reading readers, 4.0x to 6.0x, from $15.99. Reviews
show adult children buying for parents.

**28 words / 15s = 1.87 w/s.**

| TC | Audio | Visual |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:03 | **SYNC** (boy): "Read the one about the whale." | Lamp-lit chair. She opens the picture book. He leans on her knee, eager. |
| 0:03–0:06 | **VO:** "It doesn't happen all at once." | Insert: the page is a blur. Book pushed away, drawn close. Neither resolves. |
| 0:06–0:08 | **SYNC** (grandmother): "Maybe tomorrow, hm?" | Book closes. His face falls. She looks away toward the lamp. |
| 0:08–0:11 | **SYNC** (daughter, off): "Try these, Mum." | A hand offers the folded glasses. She unfolds them, spring hinge flexing, puts them on. Insert: the page SNAPS sharp in a single frame. |
| 0:11–0:15 | **VO:** "All the way to six. grinderPUNCH. Fifteen ninety-nine." | She reads, held close, eyes bright. He leans in. Hard cut at 0:13 to the real product hero card. **TEXT:** `4.0x – 6.0x` / `FROM $15.99` |

**What makes it work.** The low point is a kind lie, not a complaint. The product
arrives in the purchaser's hand, so the ad shows the buyer their own role. The
escalation "all the way to six" argues strength without an unverifiable
comparison. And the boy leaning in at 0:11 is the proof beat, delivered without a
word.

**One accuracy note that matters.** At 4x to 6x the focal distance is roughly 20
centimetres, so the book must be held close in every beat. Show it at arm's
length and anyone who has used strong readers sees the lie immediately. Check
this class of physical detail for every product — getting it right is most of
what "professional" means here.

## Beat-writing craft

**The low point carries the ad.** Write it first, then build backwards. If you
cannot describe it as a single facial expression, the arc is wrong.

**Show the failure, do not narrate it.** The blur is on screen; the VO says what
it means. Never let VO describe what the picture already shows.

**The turn is one frame.** Snap, not dissolve. Say "in a single frame."

**Give the offer 2 seconds minimum.** Cut to the hero card at 0:13, not 0:14.

**The last line is brand plus one number.** Nothing else.

**Dialogue should sound overheard, not delivered.** "Maybe tomorrow, hm?" reads
as a real person. "I just can't see the words anymore" reads as a script.

## What fails

- **A narrator explaining a feeling the face is already showing.** Redundancy
  reads as an ad; restraint reads as a film.
- **A monologue.** If one character talks for 15 seconds you have made a UGC
  spot with a 3D skin on it. Use the arcs.
- **Three or more characters.** No room at 15 seconds. Two, or one plus a hand.
- **A montage.** Multiple locations in 15 seconds destroys the key frame lock and
  the emotional through-line. One room.
- **Charm with no argument.** Warmth is the delivery mechanism, not the product.
  The VO still has to say what it is and what it costs.
- **A product that failed Gate 0.** No arc rescues a spec-sheet benefit.
